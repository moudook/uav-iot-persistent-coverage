Abstract—UAV swarms can provide temporary wireless cover-
age for Internet-of-Things (IoT) deployments in areas where fixed
infrastructure is unavailable, damaged, or insufficient. Persistent
operation is considerably harder than one-shot deployment be-
cause coverage, inter-UAV connectivity, battery depletion, limited
charging resources, and UAV failures are tightly coupled. This
paper formulates the problem of maintaining at least K UAVs
over every ground sensor while preserving a connected swarm
and respecting finite energy and safety constraints. The resulting
decision process is modelled as a cooperative Decentralized Par-
tially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP). We
propose a Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)
controller under centralized training and decentralized execution
(CTDE). Each UAV executes a shared policy from local observa-
tions, while a centralized critic is used only during training. The
learned controller is combined with an explicit feasibility mask,
a reserve-UAV recovery mechanism, and a small infrastructure-
side charging arbitration procedure. A focused survey of ten
closely related studies shows that coverage, connectivity, energy-
aware operation, recharge scheduling, and fault recovery have
largely been studied separately or in different combinations.
The proposed framework targets their intersection and defines
an evaluation protocol covering persistent coverage, connectivity
availability, recovery delay, energy consumption, charging con-
tention, safety, and scalability. Numerical performance claims
are intentionally deferred until the proposed experiments are
completed.
Index Terms—UAV-IoT networks, persistent coverage, fault
tolerance, multi-agent reinforcement learning, MAPPO, Dec-
POMDP, connectivity maintenance, energy-aware coordination.
I. INTRODUCTION
The Internet of Things (IoT) increasingly operates in envi-
ronments such as agricultural fields, disaster-affected regions,
industrial sites, and remote monitoring areas where fixed
communication infrastructure may be sparse or temporar-
ily unavailable. UAVs can provide rapidly deployable aerial
communication or sensing support because their positions
can be changed in response to the spatial distribution of
demand. Energy-efficient UAV control has therefore been stud-
ied for communication coverage, fairness, and connectivity,
while aerial-base-station placement has been formulated as a
learning-based spatial optimization problem [1], [2].
The practical difficulty changes substantially when the
network must remain operational for a long mission rather
than only achieve good coverage at one instant. The sup-
plied project model considers N UAVs operating over M
ground sensors, finite batteries, communication links between
neighboring UAVs, a limited number of charging pads, and
the possibility that an active UAV fails during the mission.
The central requirement is persistent redundancy: every sensor
should remain within coverage range of at least K active
UAVs. At the same time, the UAV communication graph
should remain connected so that information can be forwarded
through the swarm to a ground station or data sink.
These requirements conflict. A UAV that moves toward an
uncovered sensor may leave a coverage gap elsewhere. A
UAV that returns to charge may be the only communication
bridge between two groups of UAVs. A failure removes
both its coverage contribution and its communication links,
requiring the remaining swarm to reconfigure. Charging is
also a shared resource: several UAVs can become energy-
critical simultaneously while only a small number of pads are
available. Persistent service therefore requires a joint decision
process rather than a sequence of unrelated coverage and
charging heuristics.
A centralized controller faces a rapidly growing joint action
space. In the proposed discrete action model, each UAV selects
one of six actions: hover, move in one of four cardinal
directions, or charge. The joint action space consequently
contains 6N combinations. This growth does not by itself
make centralized learning impossible for every small swarm,
but it makes explicit joint-action enumeration and centralized
policy design progressively less attractive as N increases. A
decentralized execution architecture is therefore desirable for
scalability and fault containment.
Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) provides a
natural framework for this setting. CTDE allows a central-
ized critic to use information unavailable to the individual
UAV during training while retaining decentralized policies
during deployment. MAPPO is particularly suitable because
it combines centralized value estimation during training with

The research question addressed in this work is: Can a

The contributions are:
1) A unified system model coupling persistent K-coverage,
inter-UAV connectivity, finite battery energy, charging
contention, and UAV failure recovery.
2) A CTDE MAPPO framework with parameter-shared
decentralized actors, a centralized training critic, hard
action feasibility masks, and an explicit reserve-UAV
recovery mechanism.
3) A focused literature synthesis showing the differ-
ence between existing coverage, connectivity, recharge-
scheduling, and fault-tolerant approaches and the com-
bined problem considered here.
4) A benchmark and metric protocol designed to sepa-
rate coverage, connectivity, energy, recovery, safety, and
scalability effects rather than relying only on a single
aggregate reward.

II. LITERATURE SURVEY
The survey is restricted to work that is directly related to
at least one of four components of the target problem: UAV
coverage/control, inter-agent connectivity, persistent energy-
aware operation, or fault-tolerant swarm recovery. The ten
core papers in Table I are discussed comparatively rather
than as isolated summaries. MAPPO is cited separately as the
underlying learning method [3].
A further recent line of work integrates communication and
control directly within MARL. MAPPO itself is adopted from
Yu et al. [3]. Sun et al. formulate joint resource allocation and
3D trajectory control and use action masking for constrained
hybrid actions [11]. Carvalho and Aguiar address decen-
tralized multi-agent coverage path planning with parameter
sharing, CTDE, safety filtering, and zero-shot generalization
[13]. Zhuang Chen et al. formulate UAV trajectory design
for emergency communication as a constrained MDP and
explicitly account for energy and turning behavior [12]. These
works reinforce the practicality of CTDE, action masking, and
structured rewards, but their primary objectives differ from
persistent redundant coverage with explicit failure recovery.

III. SYSTEM MODEL
A. System Architecture
The system contains N active UAVs, one reserve UAV,
M fixed IoT sensors, a ground data sink/base station, and
S charging pads distributed within the operating region Ω.
UAVs provide sensor coverage through a range-limited air-
to-ground link and form a multi-hop communication network
among themselves. Sensor data reaches the sink through the
UAV communication graph whenever the graph is connected.
For tractability, the UAVs fly at a common nominal altitude
z0, so the decision process operates in the horizontal plane

An action is masked if it violates a locally predictable hard

perfectly predictable from one-hop observations; instead, the
actor receives the local bridge-risk feature ρi and the team

K. Failure and Recovery
A failure event changes Fi from 0 to 1 at the detection
instant tf. One reserve UAV is maintained with sufficient
energy for deployment when feasible. After a detected failure,
the reserve is activated and the active UAVs continue using
their decentralized policies. The recovery time is defined as
τf = inf {τ ≥0 : nd(tf + τ) ≥K, ∀d} ,
(20)
when recovery is achieved before the evaluation horizon. If
recovery is impossible under the available fleet, the event is
reported as an unrecovered failure rather than assigning an
artificial recovery time.
A recovery-time guarantee is therefore conditional: the
reserve mechanism can reduce the time required for reconfig-
uration, but it cannot mathematically guarantee restoration for
arbitrary sensor layouts, fleet sizes, or multiple simultaneous
failures. This distinction is important for a realistic system
model.

B. Action Feasibility Mask
Before the sampled action is executed, the simulator eval-
uates the predicted next position and applies the following
checks: workspace bounds, maximum speed, minimum UAV
separation, charge validity, and connectivity-preservation con-
straints. If a candidate movement would disconnect the com-
munication graph, it is masked in normal operation. During
an active recovery event, the connectivity rule remains a hard
constraint unless the simulator enters a predefined recovery
mode in which temporary reconnection is required by the
recovery objective.
This separation between policy learning and hard feasibility
is important. The neural network learns how to trade off
several feasible actions, while the safety layer prevents the
network from selecting actions that violate non-negotiable
physical constraints.
C. Failure-Aware Execution
When a failure event is detected, the environment removes
the failed UAV from the active graph, activates the reserve
UAV if the reserve is available, and updates the local obser-
vations. The reserve UAV uses the same shared actor after
insertion into the mission. The other UAVs also re-evaluate
their local bridge-risk and coverage information. No prediction
of the failure event is required; the controller reacts to the
observed state change.
D. Mapping from Model to Algorithm
The algorithm is deliberately aligned with the system model.
Sensor positions produce the coverage quantities in (7)–(10);
neighboring UAV positions produce the communication graph
in (11); battery and action information produce the energy
state in (14); charging requests enter the assignment problem
in (18); and failure indicators activate the recovery process
in (20). Thus, every actor input corresponds to an explicitly
defined state quantity and every learned action has a defined
environmental effect.
E. Training and Generalization Protocol
Training episodes should randomize sensor layouts, initial
UAV positions, initial battery levels, charging-pad locations
within the allowed region, and failure times. A simple curricu-
lum can be used: first train under no failures, then introduce
single failures, and finally introduce simultaneous charging
contention and near-disconnection scenarios. The final model
should be evaluated on held-out random seeds and scenario
configurations not used for parameter tuning.
A practical implementation can use a multilayer perceptron
for the shared actor and centralized critic with normalized
numerical features and masked action logits. If fixed-length
neighbor features are insufficient, a permutation-invariant
pooling operation or a small attention encoder can be added
without changing the underlying Dec-POMDP formulation.
A recurrent policy should be introduced only if experiments
show that instantaneous observations do not provide sufficient
temporal information.

The first experiment varies sensor density while keeping the
fleet fixed. The second varies fleet size to examine scalability.
The third varies battery capacity and charge rate. The fourth
varies the number of charging pads. The fifth varies Rcomm
and Rcov to create well-connected, marginally connected, and
difficult coverage geometries. The sixth injects single-UAV
failures at early, middle, and late mission times and at both
central and peripheral locations.
For each scenario, the same random seeds and initial con-
ditions must be used for all comparison models. At least five
independent seeds are recommended for each configuration,
with the mean and sample standard deviation reported. When
space permits, confidence intervals should also be shown. Raw
reward curves may be included as supplementary evidence, but

metrics in Table IX.
Ablation experiments should separately remove: (i) the
connectivity-criticality feature/reward term, (ii) the reserve-
UAV mechanism, (iii) charging arbitration, and (iv) action
masking. This identifies whether each proposed component
contributes a measurable effect. In particular, the comparison
between the full proposed framework and vanilla MAPPO is
necessary to distinguish improvements caused by MAPPO it-

design.

VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper defines a persistent and fault-tolerant UAV-IoT
coverage problem in which the swarm must jointly man-
age redundant sensor coverage, inter-UAV connectivity, finite
battery energy, charging contention, and UAV failures. The
proposed solution uses MAPPO under centralized training


=== PAGE 10 ===
and decentralized execution, complemented by explicit fea-
sibility masking, infrastructure-side charging arbitration, and
a reserve-UAV recovery mechanism.
The formulation is intentionally implementable. The learned
actors operate on quantities that a physical UAV can measure
or estimate locally, while the simulator computes the corre-
sponding global quantities for training and evaluation. The
literature survey indicates that the required components exist
individually across coverage, connectivity, recharge schedul-
ing, and fault-tolerant MARL studies, but the combined
problem formulation remains a meaningful integration target.
The experimental phase should therefore focus on whether
the proposed combination produces measurable improvements
in persistent coverage and recovery while keeping energy,
charging, movement, and safety costs under control.
No performance advantage is claimed in this report before
the experiments are executed. Once the implementation is
complete, the same metric and comparison structure can be
used to report both absolute results and trade-offs, including
the possible cost of more conservative movement in exchange
for improved connectivity robustness or faster recovery.

%% MAIN

The rule baselines separate sharply, and the ordering is informative.  The
Fixed formation, which never moves, covers $0.372$ of the sensor-steps:
it is the floor that the initial deployment alone provides, and it is also
the controller that never satisfies both invariants at once.  The Greedy
recharge controller, which returns to a pad when its battery falls below a
threshold and otherwise holds station, reaches $0.786$ by trading
coverage for energy.  The Reactive heuristic, which moves the swarm towards
the uncovered sensors, reaches $0.953$ with full connectivity and a
service continuity of $0.751$, and it is the strongest of the three
classic rules.

The learned controller does not reach the heuristic.  The strongest
controller in the table is the unprivileged reactive rule, at $0.972$, and
the gap to it is $0.271$ of persistent coverage; the 95\% interval on
that gap, from the learned controller's spread over its 5 training
runs, is $[0.205, 0.337]$.  The scene-to-scene variation does not enter this
interval, because every controller is evaluated on the same scenes and it
therefore cancels in the difference --- to the extent that the difficulty
of a scene enters every controller additively and with the same size,
which we state as an assumption and whose per-scene scores are in the
artefact, so that it is checkable rather than merely asserted.

The privileged reactive heuristic is also the controller with the most
information, and the comparison should be read with that in mind.  It is
given the \emph{exact} set of under-covered sensors, which corresponds to an
unbounded broadcast of the coverage state; the learned policy receives a
fixed-size summary of it.  Part of the gap between them is therefore a
difference of information rather than of control.  That is a caveat that is
easy to assert and easy to get wrong, so we measured it rather than leaving
it as one: a second version of the same rule, with the motion and recharge
logic unchanged but the global coverage state removed --- each UAV judges
only the sensors within twice the coverage radius of its own position, and
there is no assignment, so UAVs that want the same sensor contend for it
--- is reported as \emph{Reactive (local assignment)} in the table above.  It
reaches $0.972$ with connectivity availability $1.000$ and
a service continuity of $0.777$, against the privileged version's
$0.953$, $1.000$ and $0.751$.  The privileged version is in fact slightly WORSE than the unprivileged one (-0.019), so the global coverage state is not what gives the rule its edge --- the global round-robin assignment makes the UAVs contend for sensors, and the local rule does better --- and the gap to the learned controller is therefore not an information artefact at all.

The connectivity invariant deserves a sentence, because the reported
availability is below $1.000$ and the filter is claimed to guarantee
connectivity.  The guarantee is about \emph{actions}: the mask evaluates
the prospective graph for every candidate action, and the joint projection
restores the previous configuration when a set of individually admissible
actions would jointly disconnect the swarm.  The step-by-step accounting
in the artefact tests that claim over the whole evaluation matrix, and it
holds: of the $6$ transitions into a disconnected graph,
$0$ are attributable to a policy action --- every one of them is attributable to the recovery mechanism and none to a policy action, so the filter's guarantee holds over the actions and the availability below 1.000 is the price of the recovery.
The other source is the recovery mechanism: when a UAV's pack empties away
from a pad it is removed from the active set, and the swarm can be left
disconnected until the reserve UAV re-seats it at a charging pad and it
flies back.  The availability below $1.000$ is that mechanism's signature,
and it is the price of the recovery rather than a failure of the filter.

The mission-relevant quantity is the joint invariant, and it separates
the two controllers far more sharply than the coverage does.  The
reactive heuristic satisfies the coverage requirement and the connectivity requirement simultaneously in $0.751$ of the
mission, against $0.038$ for the learned controller.  The
coverage ratio is a weak proxy for that: a controller can hold a high
per-sensor coverage while never covering every sensor at the same
instant, which is what the learned controller does, and the service
continuity metric is therefore the one that distinguishes these two
controllers most sharply, even though we criticise it for a different
reason elsewhere.

It is worth separating the two sources of variation in the headline
number, because they behave differently.  Across the 5
training runs of the learned controller the coverage varies by $0.059$
(the standard error of the mean is $0.026$); across the
5 evaluation scenes it varies by $0.079$ (the
standard error of the mean over the scenes is $0.035$).  The
scene component is common to every controller in the comparison, because
they are all evaluated on the same scenes, and it therefore cancels in
every difference this paper reports; the seed component does not, and it
is the seed component that the training-seed spread measures.  A reader
comparing two controllers should read the seed component as the
uncertainty of the comparison and the scene component as a property of the
configuration.  Two caveats belong with these numbers.  The spread
quoted for a learned controller is the spread of its per-seed means,
and each per-seed mean averages five evaluation scenes of a single
rollout, so it contains the rollout-to-rollout variation of a policy
that samples at every step and not only the variation between training
runs.  And the cancellation of the scene component in a difference is an
assumption rather than an identity: it holds if the difficulty of a
scene enters every controller additively and with the same size, and
it fails if a scene is hard for one controller and easy for the other.
The per-scene scores are in the artefact, so the assumption is
checkable rather than merely asserted.

The question that this comparison invites --- whether more training would
have closed the gap --- deserves an answer rather than an assumption,
because a hand-designed rule embeds a human's solution while the learner
receives a fixed budget.  The first training run's per-episode coverage,
measured on the training scene, runs from $0.453$ to
$0.849$ over its 25 episodes, and its mean over
the first five episodes ($0.643$) is the same as its mean over
the last five ($0.687$): the curve has flattened.  The best
single episode, $0.849$, is on a training scene, and the same
policy scores $0.761$ on unseen scenes, so the training-scene
figure is not an estimate of the deployed performance.  The budget is
therefore bounded rather than eliminated: at this configuration the curve
is flat over the budget we can afford, and we do not claim that a larger
budget would not change the picture.  The sweep behind that statement
trains independent runs at each of three budgets rather than reading one
run's curve at different points, so it compares budgets and does not
restate the curve: 8 episodes: 0.609, 16 episodes: 0.640, and the coverage moves by +0.031 between the shortest and the longest budget, against a seed-to-seed spread of about 0.099, so the shortfall is not made of the training budget at this scale.

The learned controller does not reach the heuristic, and the two learned
baselines locate the reason.  The Masked PPO with a local critic differs
from the proposed method in exactly one respect: each agent has its own
actor and its own critic on its own observation, instead of a
parameter-shared actor with a centralised critic.  It reaches
$0.632$ against the proposed method's $0.701$, a difference
of $-0.068$ that is small beside the seed-to-seed spread of the
learner, and Section~\ref{sec:results_credit} tests that comparison
properly, on paired training seeds.  The Independent DQN arm, at
$0.396$, is the value-based point of comparison and shows what the
task costs a controller that does not use the framework's mechanisms at
all.  Vanilla MAPPO, which removes the
framework's observation terms, reward shaping, charging arbitration,
reserve-UAV mechanism and action mask, shows how much of the learned
method's behaviour survives without them.

\paragraph{On the evaluation protocol, which is not neutral.} The rule
baselines are deterministic, so they have no sampling step; the learned
policy is evaluated by sampling, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:policy} specifies a
sampled policy.  The sampling is seeded: the stream is derived from the
evaluation seed alone, so an evaluation is a function of (policy, scene),
every controller sees the same stream on the same scene, and the numbers in
this section can be recomputed from the stored policies.

Two further properties of the measurement are pinned rather than left to
the machine, because both of them change the result.  The floating-point
kernels differ between the CPU and the GPU, so the same seed trained on
the two gives different weights --- the difference is small, and it
compounds through the updates --- and the order of the reductions depends
on the thread count, so it changes them too.  A device chosen by how much
GPU memory happened to be free would therefore make a training run a
function of the machine's momentary load rather than of its seed, and a
variant and the arm it is compared against could end up on different
devices, which is not a paired comparison at all.  The device and the
thread count are consequently part of the protocol, every artefact records
the device it was produced on, and the audit checks that a training run is
a function of its seed.

We report the
deterministic version of the same actor as
well, and the difference is large: the argmax policy reaches $0.465$
against $0.701$ for the sampled one, although it travels a comparable
distance ($7314$~m against $9700$~m), so it is
not a static controller --- it is a different and worse one, and the
difference between them is the contribution of the exploration that the
reward does not price.  The comparison with the deterministic baselines is
therefore not like-for-like in either direction: sampling makes the learned
controller take risks it would not take deterministically, and it is scored
on the mean of a distribution of trajectories rather than on one of them.
Reporting both rows is the point.

One further number in the table is worth stating, because it disposes of a
concern that the framework raises.  The safety filter and the joint
projection together did not execute $0$ of the learned
controller's sampled actions, so the policy gradient is very nearly
consistent with the trajectory that was actually taken; the
"the executed action is not the chosen action" problem, which is real in
masked reinforcement learning, is not what limits this controller.

The learned controller also burns more energy than the heuristic
($209497$~J against $183675$~J over the same episode) while
stranding fewer UAVs, which is the signature of a policy that moves
deliberately rather than one that wanders.  Its connectivity availability is
below $1.000$ because UAVs leave the active set when a pack empties away
from a pad, and not because the safety filter leaks: over $2000$ steps of a
random policy the graph was disconnected on no step in which the active set
was unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results_abl}).

%% OBS

The three arms are cumulative, and arm~C is the proposed configuration; the
component ablation of the next section isolates the three observation terms
individually.  Arm~A is the observation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:obs} as originally
written.

Arm~A has the property that the shared-actor symmetry collapse predicts.
The deterministic policy nearly collapses to a single action --- the
``Distinct'' column counts $1.46$ distinct actions out of eight --- and
the swarm's spread is $7.9$~m, which is the radius of the initial
cluster rather than the extent of a deployment.  This is the failure that
Foerster et al.'s condition~(ii) is about: with an observation that does not
break the permutation symmetry, a parameter-shared network is a single
function of the observation, so two agents in symmetric states must receive
the same action distribution, and no amount of training can change that.
The consequence is measurable rather than theoretical, and the diagnostic
that exposes it is a count of distinct actions, which costs nothing and
which the raw coverage number does not make visible.

Arm~B adds the one-hot identity, and the condition does what it says about
specialisation: the number of distinct actions rises from $1.46$ to
$2.32$ out of eight, and the swarm disperses rather than standing
still.  Its effect on the coverage, paired over the shared training seeds,
is $-0.123$ with a 95\% interval of $[-0.477, +0.232]$, so we do
not claim that the identity buys coverage at this budget: what it buys is
the \emph{ability} to specialise, which the distinct-action count measures
directly and which the coverage does not.

Arm~C adds the broadcast coverage-map summary and the pad bearing, and it
is the configuration the paper proposes.  The spread rises to
$14.3$~m --- the swarm moves further --- while the distinct-action
count falls to $2.11$, so the policy moves more but decides more
uniformly.  That is the signature of a policy that is following the map
rather than dividing the work, and the paired effect on the coverage is
$-0.001$ (95\% interval $[-0.251, +0.249]$).

So the observation terms change what the policy \emph{does} --- how far it
travels, and whether it can specialise at all --- much more clearly than
they change the coverage it achieves.  That is worth stating, because the
alternative reading of the same table is that the observation does not
matter, and it is not what the data support.  A coverage map tells a
controller \emph{where} the work is; it does not tell it \emph{which} UAV
should do it, and turning a location into an assignment requires per-agent
credit, which a shared team reward does not supply.  The map makes the
deficiency visible, and therefore actionable-looking, and a policy without
the credit to act on it chases.

The arms share their training seeds, so the comparisons are paired: $adding the agent identity$ (-0.123, 95\% interval [-0.477, +0.231], an upper limit below the gap of 0.271); $the full observation of the proposed configuration$ (-0.001, 95\% interval [-0.251, +0.249], an upper limit below the gap of 0.271).  Each upper limit is below the gap of 0.271 to the reactive heuristic, so the content of the observation is not what accounts for the gap; what the observation terms buy is the ability to specialise at all, and the arms here do not separate the coverage they produce.

The resolution of the summary makes no difference to the coverage: $G=0$: 0.645, and the largest of the differences is 0.000, inside the seed-to-seed spread of the learner (0.058).  The map is therefore neither what makes the controller work nor what stops it working: it is a channel the policy uses to move differently, and the difference it makes is visible in the trajectory rather than in the score.

The ablation of the next section prices the observation terms individually  The reason is easy to see in
retrospect.  Without a direction to the charger a UAV cannot come home, so
the swarm drains its packs in the field; and a swarm that has drained its
packs is not a swarm that can be asked to cover anything.

%% ENERGY

The first version of this section told a different story, and the
difference is worth recording, because it is the clearest example in this
work of a diagnostic leading to a repair rather than to a conclusion.  The
step-level diagnostic of the learned policy produced an alarming number:
with the safety filter as it was, the policy stranded \emph{all eight} UAVs
in nearly every episode, while the reactive rules stranded $0.4$.  A
stranded UAV has an empty pack away from a charging pad; it can no longer
fly, it leaves the swarm, and it stops covering.  The obvious reading was
that the controller had failed at energy management, and the obvious fix
was a reward term that prices the consequence of an empty pack.

The reading was wrong, and Table~\ref{tab:results_energy} says so.  The three interventions are paired with the proposed configuration -- they share its training seeds -- so each one is reported as an interval rather than as a point, and each interval's upper limit is below the gap of 0.271 to the reactive heuristic: $removing the energy constraint$ (-0.044, 95\% interval [-0.327, +0.238], an upper limit below the gap of 0.271); $removing the threshold term$ (-0.021, 95\% interval [-0.063, +0.020], an upper limit below the gap of 0.271); $the previous energy rule$ (-0.054, 95\% interval [-0.245, +0.137], an upper limit below the gap of 0.271).  The energy model is therefore not what accounts for the gap, and the paper does not claim that it is.

What the diagnostic actually found was a defect in the safety filter rather
than a property of the policy.  The mask's energy test admitted a
departure with a flat battery, so a UAV could fly until its pack was empty
away from a pad; the correction is to require a UAV to retain, after the
move, the energy to reach a charging pad plus the reserve margin.  The
stranding falls from $6.1$ UAVs per episode to $0.9$, and
the return-to-charge transitions rise from $135$ to $636$ per
episode, because the UAVs now come home instead of dying in the field.  The
coverage is unchanged to within the spread, and that is the honest
conclusion: the defect was real, the repair was correct, and neither the
defect nor the repair explains the gap to the reactive heuristic.

It is worth being explicit about what that null is and is not about, since
a null result is easy to misread.  It is \emph{not} a statement that the
energy constraint is inactive: the two rules differ by $5.2$
stranded UAVs per episode and by $501$ return-to-charge
transitions, so the test is doing substantial work and the swarm's energy
behaviour differs markedly between them.  It is a statement about the
effect of that work on the \emph{coverage}, and the effect is small.  A
null in an arm of this width is also a statement about the resolution of
the arm and not about the component, and we report that resolution with
the interval.

That is the sequence the framework is for.  A coverage comparison on its
own would have reported that the learned controller mismanages energy, and
a reward re-weighting would have been the wrong repair.  The controlled
arms are what showed that the energy model was not the problem, and the
defect they exposed in the filter was the actual problem.  By the end of
that sequence the energy explanation had been removed from the list, and
what remained --- the credit assignment of a shared team reward --- was
neither supported nor refuted by it.

%% ABL

Every variant is trained on the same configuration with the same budget and
from the same set of 3 training seeds, and differs from the
full configuration in exactly one component, so a difference is
attributable to that component.  The budget is the base comparison's
(25 episodes), and the full configuration of this table is
the first 3 of the base comparison's training runs --- the same
policies, not a cheaper experiment --- so the two tables describe the same
learner.  Because the variants share the training seeds, the
comparison is \emph{paired}: a difference is measured on the same training
runs, and what is reported is a confidence interval for that difference
rather than a comparison of two numbers against a threshold.  A threshold
would be the wrong instrument here, and it is worth saying why, because
the older version of this table used one.  A rule that calls a difference
``measurable'' when it exceeds some other number --- the learner's own
seed-to-seed spread, say --- has a false-positive rate that depends on
the spread of that other number, and it buys nothing in power: at three
training seeds it would flag roughly one null component in three, while
still being unable to detect a true effect of the size it flags.  The
paired interval does not have that failure mode, and it also reports the
resolution of the measurement, which is the number a reader actually
needs.  no single component changes the persistent coverage by more than the seed-to-seed spread of the learner itself, and the table is reported in full because that is the result.

What the intervals do and do not say is worth stating precisely, because a
null result of this width is a statement about the resolution of the
measurement and not about the component.  The measurement resolves no
effect smaller than about $n/a$ of persistent coverage, at
this training budget and with this number of training seeds.  The
intervals are per-arm and are not adjusted for the number of arms, so a
reader who wants a family-wise statement should treat the set of arms as
one test with several comparisons rather than as several independent
tests.  The intervals do \emph{not} show that a component is unnecessary.
A component can be redundant with another, in which case removing either
one alone costs nothing while removing both costs a great deal; and a
component that matters only at a larger budget is invisible at this one.
Establishing budget-independence would need a budget sweep, which we do
not run, and we therefore do not claim it.

Two of the null results are explained rather than merely reported.  The
articulation-point term makes no measurable difference because the safety
filter already forbids any move that would create an articulation point, so
on an executed trajectory the quantity it measures is nearly constant and
the term has almost no variance to act on: the constraint is enforced by the
filter, and the reward term is a readout of it.  The degraded-service term
is close to inert for the same reason, since the filter keeps the graph
connected whenever the active set is unchanged.  In other words, the terms
that look as though they should matter most are the ones the hard
constraints have already discharged, and the terms that do matter are the
ones the filter cannot enforce: who covers what, and how the energy is
spent.

%% ABLSTRESS

The ordering of the components is not the same here as in the nominal regime, and that is a result rather than a nuisance: removing the agent identity costs 0.078, the pad bearing costs 0.070, the coverage map costs 0.045, the action mask costs 0.032 of persistent coverage, so the component helps; removing the whole design GAINS 0.193, the reserve UAV GAINS 0.187, so the component is a liability there.  The framework's recovery mechanism, which re-seats an exhausted UAV at a charging pad, is a liability when energy is scarce, because the re-seated UAV is out of communication range of the swarm until it flies back, and the swarm spends the scenario disconnected and gathered rather than spread.
